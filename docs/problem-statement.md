# Problem Statement

## Background

The PM Internship Scheme is a massive government initiative aimed at providing real-world opportunities to youth across the country. However, internship platforms typically rely on rigid keyword searches and simple filters. This approach heavily penalizes first-generation learners and candidates from rural or non-technical backgrounds who may not know the exact industry jargon to include in their resumes or search queries, despite possessing the necessary underlying skills.

## The Problem

Youth applying to the PM Internship Scheme — particularly first-generation learners from rural, tribal, and remote-college backgrounds with limited digital exposure — struggle to identify which of the hundreds of listed internships actually match their skills, interests, and location. This leads to misaligned applications and missed opportunities. 

Simultaneously, recruiters are forced to sift through thousands of mismatched applications without an easy way to understand why a candidate might be a good fit beyond a basic keyword match. There is no explainable feedback loop for candidates who are rejected.

## Who is Affected

- **Candidates (First-generation learners / Job Seekers):** Candidates who lack strong digital literacy or formal professional networks. They face language barriers and lack a trusted way to verify informally-acquired skills.
- **Employers (Companies / Recruiters):** HR teams looking for genuine talent but overwhelmed by the volume of irrelevant applications because of a broken matching system.

## Why It Matters

The cost of this problem is deeply societal. When capable candidates are locked out of opportunities meant specifically for them, the scheme's core objective fails. It results in a system where opportunity exists in abundance, but access to the *right* opportunity is artificially restricted by a poor search experience. For employers, it translates to hundreds of wasted hours reviewing poor-fit applications.

## Why Existing Solutions Fall Short

Existing platforms rely heavily on exact keyword matching and Boolean searches. They do not account for semantic similarities (e.g., matching "data analysis" with "data mining"), spelling mistakes, or nuanced geographical constraints (e.g., commute viability). Furthermore, current platforms function as "black boxes" — they give a list of jobs but never explain *why* the job is a match, making it hard for low-confidence candidates to trust the system or understand where they need to upskill.
